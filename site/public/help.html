What ClearToShip actually checks
— no jargon.

If you used Cursor, Claude Code, v0 or Lovable to build this and a scan came back with findings you don't understand, you're the reader this page is for. Every category below is the same thing the tool checks — just explained the way you'd explain it to a co-founder, not a security team.

First, the basics

What is a "security scanner," really?

It's a program that reads your code the way a careful reviewer would, looking for a specific list of mistakes that are common, well understood, and usually invisible until someone exploits them. It doesn't run your app or guess at your intentions — it looks for patterns: a database table anyone can read, a password typed directly into a file, a button that saves data without checking who clicked it.

You don't need to know security to use the results. Every finding names the exact file and line, explains what's wrong in one sentence, and — for the checks the free tier covers — --fix-prompt turns it into an instruction you can hand straight back to whatever AI tool wrote the code.

Reading a result

What "Critical," "High," "Medium" and "Low" mean

Not every finding is equally urgent. Severity is ClearToShip's best guess at how bad it would be if this specific gap were the one someone found first.

Critical — someone could take or destroy real data or money today, with no special access High — a real weakness, but it needs some extra condition to be exploited Medium — worth fixing before it matters, not an emergency Low — a defense-in-depth gap, not a live hole

The default rule of thumb: don't launch with an open Critical. Everything else can usually wait for the next release.

The six questions it's actually asking

Every finding is one of these, underneath

The technical names for these (OWASP Top 10, CWE numbers, rule IDs like CTS001) are in the full rule tables and the standards explainer, for anyone who wants them. Here's what each one is actually asking, in order.

🚪 Can a stranger see or change data they shouldn't?

This is the single most common gap. An AI coding tool will happily write a button that saves, deletes, or updates something — and forget to check who is clicking it, or whether the data belongs to them at all.

Like a filing cabinet with everyone's records in it, and the lock is on the room, not the individual drawers — anyone who gets in can pull any folder.
Technical names: Broken Access Control, missing Row Level Security, IDOR

🔑 Are your passwords and secret keys out in the open?

A "key" here means a password-like string your app uses to talk to another service — your database, Stripe, OpenAI. If one of these ends up typed directly into your code, committed to git, or sent to the browser, anyone who sees that code (or your site's source) can use it as if they were you.

Like leaving the office key taped under the doormat — convenient for you, and for whoever else finds it.
Technical names: Hardcoded secrets, exposed credentials, client-side key leakage

💉 Could someone sneak instructions into your app?

This is what happens when your app takes something a stranger typed — a search box, a form, a URL — and treats part of it as a command instead of plain text. The classic version targets your database; a newer version targets your AI model, by hiding instructions inside text it's asked to read.

Like a note that says "and also, unlock the safe" slipped into a stack of paperwork someone was told to process without reading closely.
Technical names: SQL Injection, Cross-Site Scripting, Prompt Injection

📦 Are you accidentally installing something malicious?

AI tools sometimes invent a package name that sounds right but doesn't exist — and someone else can register that exact name on npm with real malware inside, betting that it'll get installed by accident. This checks whether every package you depend on is real, spelled correctly, and not one of these known traps.

Like ordering a part by a name your supplier misheard, and a scammer happens to sell something under that exact misheard name.
Technical names: Dependency/supply-chain risk, hallucinated packages, typosquatting

🤖 Could your AI feature be tricked, or run up a huge bill?

If your app calls an AI model or lets an AI agent take actions on its own (send email, delete a record, spend money), this asks three things: can outside text hijack what the model does, is there anything stopping a single request from costing you $500 in API usage, and does anything just trust what the model says without a person checking first.

Like hiring a very fast, very literal assistant, handing them your credit card, and never telling them there's a spending limit.
Technical names: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications — Excessive Agency, Unbounded Consumption, Misinformation

🚨 What happens when something goes wrong?

Every app eventually gets an error, a weird input, or a request it wasn't expecting. This checks whether that failure is handled safely — and whether anyone would even find out it happened. A security-relevant error that's silently swallowed, or that fails by opening the door instead of closing it, is its own kind of gap.

Like a fire alarm that's wired to turn itself off the moment it starts going off.
Technical names: Logging & Alerting Failures, Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions

Common questions

Before you run it

Do I need a security background to use this?

No. That's what this page is for. Every finding names a file, a line, and one plain sentence about what's wrong — you don't need to know the OWASP category to act on it.

Does it upload my code anywhere?

No. It runs entirely on your machine or CI runner and reads your files locally. The only network calls it makes (and only when you're online) are anonymous lookups of package names — never file contents, never your code — to check whether a dependency exists and is safe. --offline turns even that off. See SECURITY.md for exactly what leaves your machine and how to verify it yourself.

I don't understand a specific finding. What do I do?

Run it with --fix-prompt — it turns the finding into an instruction you can paste directly back into Cursor, Claude Code, or whatever tool wrote the code, and have it explain and fix the specific line for you.

Will this slow down my launch?

It's designed not to. A typical scan finishes in under 30 seconds, needs no account, and no setup beyond running one command.

What's "OWASP," and why does every finding mention it?

OWASP is a nonprofit that maintains a public, community-written list of the most common ways real applications get broken into. ClearToShip doesn't invent its own categories — every finding is checked against that published list (and a companion list specific to AI/LLM apps), so the labels mean the same thing here as they do everywhere else in the industry. The full breakdown of both lists, still in plain language, is in the standards explainer if you want it.

Some checks say "Pro" — what's the difference?

The dependency, secrets, and error-handling checks above are free forever, no account needed. The database-permissions, Server-Action-authorization, and AI/agent checks are the ones that catch the most severe issues most often — those are the licensed Pro tier. See the main page for what's included in each.
